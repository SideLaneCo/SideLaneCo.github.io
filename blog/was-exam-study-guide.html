What's Actually on the WAS Exam

Side Lane Co

IAAP's Web Accessibility Specialist (WAS) exam is the natural next step after CPACC, and the mistake people make is studying for it the same way: memorizing definitions. WAS assumes you already build for the web. It's 75 questions in two hours, and it tests whether you can actually apply WCAG, ARIA, and testing practice to real code, not just recite what the terms mean.

The real domain weighting

DomainWeight
Creating Accessible Web Content40%
Testing and Evaluation30%
Remediation and Best Practices30%

The largest domain by far is about actually building accessible content: semantic HTML, correct ARIA usage, keyboard operability, forms, tables, and multimedia. That's 40% of the exam on its own, more than either of the other two domains alone.

Where people underprepare

Testing and remediation together make up 60% of the exam, and they're the two areas people study the least, because they feel less like "facts to memorize" and more like judgment calls. Knowing that automated tools only catch roughly 30-40% of real accessibility issues, and knowing what to do about the other 60-70%, is exactly the kind of question WAS tests and a definitions-only study approach misses.

The trap in the wrong answers

A recurring WAS exam pattern: a broken code snippet, and several corrected versions to choose from, where the wrong answers each fix the surface symptom without fixing the real accessibility barrier. Changing an error message to red text instead of fixing its actual contrast ratio is the kind of fix that looks right and isn't, and it's exactly the sort of distinction the exam is built to catch.

The WAS Study Guide covers all three domains at their real weighting, with 20 practice questions split to match, built for people who already build for the web and want real exam prep instead of a beginner's introduction to accessibility.
